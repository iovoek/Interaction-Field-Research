The Drug Industry's 90% Problem

The pharmaceutical industry spends $2.5 billion to bring a single drug to market. Nine out of ten fail in clinical trials. This is not bad luck, and it is not greed. It is a structural consequence of analyzing disease from the wrong direction.

By Glen Brackmann with AI research assistance  |  2026  |  Exchange Is the Equation

If you wanted to design a system that maximized the amount of money spent on drug development while minimizing the number of drugs that actually worked, you would build something that looks almost exactly like the current pharmaceutical R&D pipeline. You would start with a single molecular target, build a compound to hit that target, test the compound in simplified cell cultures and animal models, and then spend a billion dollars finding out whether it works in actual human beings. It usually does not.

The 90% clinical trial failure rate is one of the most cited statistics in medicine. It is usually framed as a problem of complexity, of risk, of the inherent difficulty of biology. These framings are not wrong. But they are incomplete. The Backwards Problem framework offers a more precise diagnosis: the pharmaceutical industry is analyzing disease from the surface inward, and the 90% failure rate is the mathematically predictable consequence of that misorientation.

The Numbers First

90%
Clinical trial failure rate across all disease areas (Sun et al., 2022)
$2.5B
Average cost to bring a single drug to market, including the cost of failures
~2M
Clinical trial participants per year exposed to compounds that will ultimately fail

These numbers are not independent. The $2.5 billion figure is so high precisely because it includes the cost of the nine failures for every one success. The 2 million trial participants per year are not a side note. They are the human cost of the structural misorientation. They are the Sacrificial Mass.

The Sacrificial Mass is the term this framework uses for the waste that is structurally guaranteed by the Backwards Problem. In social media, described in Article 3, the Sacrificial Mass is adolescent mental health and democratic polarization. In pharmaceutical R&D, the Sacrificial Mass is more immediate and more personal. It is the patient in a Phase III trial who receives a compound that had no realistic chance of working. It is the disease that never receives adequate research attention because the development pipeline is clogged with expensive failures. It is the decade of delay between when a disease becomes tractable and when a working treatment reaches patients.

The 90% failure rate is not a problem of complexity. It is a problem of direction. The industry is analyzing disease from the surface inward, and the failure rate is what that looks like.

What Target-Based Discovery Actually Does

To understand the problem, you need to understand the dominant paradigm in pharmaceutical R&D: target-based drug discovery. The logic is straightforward. You identify a molecular target, usually a protein, that is implicated in a disease. You design a compound that binds to or inhibits that target. You test whether the compound works.

This is the surface-first approach. The molecular target is a surface variable: it is measurable, it is specific, it is tractable. The deep structure is the disease as a system-level phenomenon, a complex network of biological interactions that produces pathological outcomes through emergent behavior that cannot be predicted from any single component.

The problem is that most diseases are not caused by a single molecular defect. They are caused by the breakdown of complex biological systems. Cancer is not a single mutation. It is a failure of cellular regulatory networks. Alzheimer's is not just amyloid plaques. It is a failure of neuronal maintenance and clearance systems. Type 2 diabetes is not just insulin resistance. It is a failure of metabolic regulatory feedback loops. When you design a drug to hit a single target in a single pathway, you are intervening at the surface of a system whose deep structure you have not characterized. The drug may hit its target perfectly and still fail to affect the disease, because the disease is not located at that target. It is located in the interaction field of the whole system.

Target-Based (Current Standard)

Identify a molecular target. Design a compound to hit it. Test in simplified models. Discover in Phase III that the disease is not located at that target. Fail. Repeat.

Phenotypic / Systems-First (Proposed)

Observe the disease as a system-level phenomenon. Identify compounds that shift the system's behavior. Understand the mechanism after the fact. Begin from the deep structure.

This is not a hypothetical distinction. The historical data supports it. A 2022 analysis by Vincent et al. in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery found that phenotypic screening approaches, which observe complex biological function rather than isolated molecular targets, have higher clinical success rates than target-based approaches. The most successful drugs of the past two decades were discovered phenotypically and had their mechanisms elucidated only after they were already working. The industry discovered the right answer first and explained it second. That is the correct direction.

The Interaction Field Applied to Biology

The Interaction Field framework, described in detail in Article 2, provides a mathematical language for this distinction. Applied to biological systems, the core insight is that disease is not a property of individual molecules. It is a property of the interaction field that those molecules participate in.

Interaction Field Applied to Disease
V(system, t) = V_max × [1 / (1 + e^(-k(n_pathological(t) - n_c)))]
Where V(system, t) is the degree of pathological expression at time t. n_pathological(t) is the cumulative count of pathological transactions (aberrant cellular interactions) up to time t. n_c is the critical threshold at which the system tips from compensation to overt disease. k is the steepness of the transition. V_max is the maximum pathological expression.

The logistic form captures something important about disease progression that the target-based model misses entirely: the inflection point. Most diseases do not progress linearly. They progress slowly, then suddenly. There is a period of subclinical pathology during which the system is compensating, followed by a transition point at which compensation fails and overt disease emerges. The inflection point n_c is where the system tips.

Target-based drug discovery cannot identify this inflection point because it operates at the wrong level of analysis. It is looking at individual molecular interactions when the relevant phenomenon is a system-level transition. A drug that inhibits a single pathway may delay the accumulation of pathological transactions slightly, but if it does not shift the inflection point n_c, it will not change the disease trajectory in any clinically meaningful way. This is why so many drugs that work perfectly in cell culture and animal models fail in human trials: the simplified models do not have the interaction field complexity required to express the inflection point. The drug looks like it works because the system it is being tested in is too simple to fail in the way the real disease fails.

The Inflection Point as a Drug Target

The Interaction Field framework suggests a different approach to drug discovery: instead of targeting individual molecules, target the inflection point of the disease's interaction field. The inflection point is the critical number of pathological transactions at which the biological system tips from compensation to overt disease. Intervening at this inflection point requires the least therapeutic effort for the greatest effect.

This is what phenotypic screening does, implicitly, when it works. It observes complex biological systems and identifies compounds that shift the system's behavior at the level where the disease actually lives. The mechanism of action is secondary. The system-level effect is primary. This is the correct orientation: deep structure first, surface mechanism second.

The Human Cost

The Sacrificial Mass in pharmaceutical R&D has three components, and they are worth naming precisely rather than treating as abstract statistics.

The trial participants. Approximately 2 million people per year participate in clinical trials for compounds that will ultimately fail. These are not people who volunteered for a long shot. Many of them are patients with serious diseases who enrolled in a trial because they had run out of other options. The informed consent process tells them the compound might not work. It does not tell them that the compound's failure was structurally predictable from the moment the development program was designed. That is a different kind of information, and the industry does not have a mechanism for communicating it.

The delayed treatments. Every failed development program consumes resources, regulatory attention, and scientific talent that could have been directed toward approaches with a higher probability of success. The 7-to-10-year average delay per disease from failed development programs is not just a financial cost. It is a measure of how long patients with that disease waited for a treatment that the industry was theoretically capable of developing but did not, because the pipeline was clogged with expensive failures from the wrong direction.

The diseases that do not get researched. The $2.5 billion average cost per successful drug is not a fixed cost of doing science. It is a cost that is inflated by the 90% failure rate. If the failure rate were 50%, the cost per successful drug would be dramatically lower, and the industry could afford to pursue diseases that are currently considered too risky or too small a market to justify the investment. The Backwards Problem does not just waste money on diseases that are being researched. It prevents research on diseases that are not being researched because the economics do not work at a 90% failure rate.

The Sacrificial Mass in Human Terms

The pharmaceutical industry does not need to sacrifice patients to make money. It sacrifices patients because its analytical framework is oriented incorrectly. The companies that reorient, that begin with complex biological function rather than isolated molecular targets, will not only save more lives. They will make more money, because their development programs will fail less often, cost less per success, and reach patients faster. This is the same structural argument as the social media case: the Backwards Problem is not just bad for users. It is bad for the companies practicing it.

The Evidence for Reorientation

The historical record of drug discovery provides direct empirical support for the systems-first approach. The most successful drug classes of the past 30 years were not discovered by identifying a target and designing a compound to hit it. They were discovered by observing complex biological function and finding compounds that shifted it.

Statins were discovered through phenotypic screening of fungal metabolites for cholesterol-lowering activity. The mechanism was elucidated afterward. Aspirin was used for decades before anyone understood how it worked at a molecular level. The most effective antidepressants were discovered through clinical observation of unexpected effects in patients being treated for other conditions. In each case, the deep structure, the system-level effect, was identified first. The surface mechanism was explained second. This is the correct direction.

The recent success of GLP-1 agonists in obesity and metabolic disease follows the same pattern. The compounds were not designed to hit a specific obesity target. They were observed to produce system-level metabolic effects that turned out to be clinically transformative. The mechanism is still being fully characterized. The clinical effect was real and reproducible long before the mechanism was understood.

The Vincent et al. (2022) analysis in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery found that phenotypic screening approaches have higher clinical success rates than target-based approaches across multiple disease areas. The Sadri (2023) analysis in the Journal of Medicinal Chemistry found that target-based drug discovery has a fundamental efficiency problem that is not solvable by better target selection, because the problem is not the targets. The problem is the direction of analysis.

The Structural Correction

The correction is not to abandon molecular biology. It is to reorient the sequence of analysis. The current sequence is: identify target, design compound, test in simplified models, discover in clinical trials whether the target was relevant. The proposed sequence is: observe disease in complex, patient-derived models, identify compounds that shift the system's behavior, then characterize the mechanism.

This is not a new idea. It is the approach that produced the most successful drugs in history. What is new is the theoretical framework that explains why it works: the Interaction Field. The deep structure of a disease is its interaction field, the network of biological transactions that produces pathological outcomes. Intervening at the deep structure, at the level of the interaction field rather than at the level of individual molecular targets, is the correct orientation.

The Leroy Hood systems medicine framework at the Institute for Systems Biology, the Human Phenotype Ontology developed by Peter Robinson, and the P4 medicine initiative (predictive, preventive, personalized, participatory) all represent movement in this direction. They are not yet the dominant paradigm. But the 90% failure rate is a powerful argument for change, and the Interaction Field framework provides the theoretical language to explain why the change is necessary and what direction it should take.

Testable Prediction

Drug discovery programs initiating with phenotypic screening in complex, patient-derived models will exhibit a significantly lower attrition rate in Phase II and Phase III clinical trials compared to programs starting with isolated target-based screening. Specifically, the framework predicts that phenotypic-first programs will achieve Phase II success rates above 50% (vs. the current 30%) and Phase III success rates above 70% (vs. the current 50%), because they begin from the deep structure rather than the variable surface. This is a falsifiable prediction that could be tested through retrospective analysis of existing development program data.

The Same Structural Error

The pharmaceutical case and the social media case, described in Article 3, are structurally identical. In both cases, a complex system with a deep structure is being analyzed from the surface inward. In both cases, the surface variable is measurable and tractable. In both cases, the deep structure is harder to characterize but is where the relevant phenomenon actually lives. In both cases, optimizing for the surface variable produces compounding error. In both cases, the Sacrificial Mass, the human cost of the structural misorientation, is real and quantifiable.

This is the central claim of the Orientation Conjecture: that the direction of analysis matters structurally, not just practically. Starting from the deep structure and working outward produces lower error accumulation than starting from the surface and working inward. This is not a domain-specific observation. It is a structural prediction that applies wherever the surface and deep structure of a complex system diverge, which is everywhere that complex systems exist.

The pharmaceutical industry is one of the clearest examples because the cost of the error is so precisely quantifiable. $2.5 billion per successful drug. 90% failure rate. 2 million trial participants per year. 7-to-10-year delays. These are not the costs of doing difficult science. They are the costs of doing science from the wrong direction.
