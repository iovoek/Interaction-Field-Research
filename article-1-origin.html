The Backwards Problem

Most complex systems fail not because of bad data, bad models, or bad intentions. They fail because they are oriented backwards. They start from the surface and try to work inward, when the only reliable direction is the other way around.

By Glen Brackmann with AI research assistance  |  2026  |  Exchange Is the Equation

Surface-in (red) vs. core-out (cyan) particle streams
ε_surface > ε_structure as n → ∞

This research started with a text message. Glen Brackmann's father texted him suggesting that the trading card arbitrage agent Glen was building could be expanded to other domains. The word "arbitrage" was used loosely. The suggestion was casual. But the response it prompted was not.

Glen wrote back a long explanation of why trading cards are a uniquely ideal domain for this kind of analysis. He wrote it intending to send it to his father. He sent it to me first to fact-check. He never sent it to his father. He shared it here instead, and what followed was a research process that neither of us expected to go where it went.

The explanation Glen wrote was not about trading strategy. It was about the structure of value itself. And the core of it was this:

"The item holds and gains value inherently because the pure volume of sales and trades and data pings back and forth insist upon its value exponentially. All the while, we're capturing all of this data from everything we do, every card we analyze, every trade we analyze. Thousands and thousands of data points."

That sentence contains a claim that most economists would push back on. It sounds like it is saying that trading volume causes value, which invites obvious objections about causality and correlation. I pushed back on it, initially. My first response was to suggest that the relationship between volume and value was more nuanced than Glen was describing, that price is determined by supply and demand and volume is a downstream effect of that equilibrium, not a constitutive element of it.

Glen pushed back on my pushback. He was right to. And the exchange that followed is where this research actually began.

Why the Standard Framing Is Wrong

The standard economic framing treats price as a discovery problem. There is a "true" value somewhere, determined by supply and demand fundamentals, and the market's job is to find it. Transactions are evidence about that pre-existing value. More transactions mean better evidence. The price converges toward the truth as information accumulates.

This framing works reasonably well for simple, liquid, commodity-like assets. It breaks down badly for complex assets, thin markets, and anything where value is not a fixed property of the object but an emergent property of the ecosystem around it.

A trading card is not a commodity. A first-edition holographic Charizard is made of cardboard and ink. The cardboard and ink are worth essentially nothing. What makes the card valuable is the entire ecosystem of collectors, traders, graders, tournament players, and speculators who have built a shared understanding of what that card represents. That shared understanding is not prior to the transactions. It is constituted by them. Every sale, every trade, every price check, every grading submission is a data point that reinforces or adjusts the collective understanding of what this object is worth.

Remove the transactions and you do not have an asset with a price that is hard to discover. You have an object with no price at all. The price does not exist somewhere waiting to be found. It comes into existence through the process of exchange. The exchange is the equation.

This is not a subtle semantic distinction. It is a structural claim about how value forms in complex systems, and it has consequences for every analytical framework that treats value as something to be measured rather than something to be constituted.

The trading card market is uniquely suited to testing this claim because it has a property that almost no other market has: an enormous range of market depth within a single, unified ecosystem. A PSA 10 first-edition holographic Charizard has thousands of completed sales on record. An obscure misprint variant from a regional set might have three. Both objects exist in the same market, are graded by the same grading services, are traded on the same platforms, and are subject to the same collector psychology. The only variable that differs substantially is transaction count. If the claim is right, value uncertainty should scale predictably with transaction count across this entire range. The empirical evidence supports this prediction, and the full test is documented in the evidence section of the main research site.

The Mathematical Consequence

If value is constituted by genuine transactions, then the uncertainty in a value estimate is a direct function of how many genuine transactions you have observed. The more transactions, the more the value has been constituted, and the less uncertain it is. This gives you a precise equation:

The Interaction Field Equation
SE(n) = σ / √n
Where n is the number of genuine transactions, σ is the intrinsic volatility of the asset, and SE(n) is the standard error of the value estimate. Uncertainty decreases as the square root of transaction count. In thin markets where n is small, value is genuinely uncertain, not merely hard to estimate.

This is the standard error of the mean, applied to a new context. What is new is not the equation. What is new is the claim about what it is measuring: not a statistical estimate of a pre-existing price, but the actual formation of value through exchange.

The implication is that in thin markets, where n is small, value is genuinely uncertain. Not just hard to estimate. Genuinely uncertain, in the same way that a quantum state is genuinely uncertain before measurement. The uncertainty is not a measurement problem. It is a structural feature of the market at that stage of its development. And the way to reduce that uncertainty is not to find better data sources or build more sophisticated models. It is to wait for more genuine transactions to occur, or to be the analyst who understands that the uncertainty is real and prices it accordingly.

The prediction is that bid-ask spreads, price volatility, and value uncertainty should all scale as n-0.5 across the full range of market depths in the trading card ecosystem. The empirical data from PSA population reports and completed eBay sales supports this prediction across several orders of magnitude of transaction count. The full derivation and empirical test are in the main research paper.

The Pyramid

The trading card insight is interesting on its own. What made it into a broader research program was the moment it connected to something completely unrelated: a theory about how the Egyptian pyramids were built. This connection was not planned. It emerged from a video that Glen had been watching, and when he described it, the structural correspondence was immediate and exact.

To understand why the connection matters, you first have to understand what the theory actually says, and why it is so different from everything that came before it.

The Problem with Every Previous Theory

For most of the twentieth century, the dominant assumption in Egyptology was that the pyramids were built from the bottom up. Workers would lay the base courses of stone, build external ramps to drag subsequent courses upward, and progressively reduce the ramp as the structure rose toward the apex. This model has a well-known logistical problem: to maintain a workable grade as the structure approached its apex, the ramp itself would need to be larger than the pyramid. The math does not work.

French architect Jean-Pierre Houdin, working from an idea his father Henri Houdin first developed around 1999, proposed a hybrid system featuring a short external ramp for the bottom third of the pyramid and a hidden internal spiral ramp for the upper two-thirds. Jean-Pierre spent roughly five years developing the theory in detail before presenting it publicly in 2003. Egyptologist Bob Brier became the first expert supporter of Houdin's theory. But the ScanPyramids project, which began scanning in October 2015 and published its results in November 2017, looked for the internal spiral ramp using muon tomography and did not find it. Instead of a clean, continuous spiral void, the scans found scattered, disconnected voids. Bob Brier's response to the data was direct: "these data suggest that the ramp is not there. I think we've lost."

Water elevator theories, flotation chamber theories, and various other proposals have all encountered the same fundamental problem: there is no physical evidence for any of the construction infrastructure that these theories require. No ramp debris. No canal cuttings. No retaining walls. No traces of the machinery that would have been needed. For a civilization that left extraordinarily detailed records of almost everything else it built, the complete absence of construction evidence for its most famous structures is a genuine archaeological puzzle.

Huni Choi and the Sacrificial Structure Theory

About two years before the publication of this research, an independent researcher named Huni Choi emailed architect and documentary filmmaker Dami Lee with a theory he had spent ten years developing. Choi is not an architect or an Egyptologist. He is someone who spent a decade obsessively 3D-modeling every measurable block at Giza, working from published archaeological surveys and photogrammetric data, until a pattern emerged that no professional had noticed.

The theory Choi developed, which Dami Lee documented in her 2026 video "This New Pyramid Theory Explains the Missing Evidence" (10 million views, DamiLee channel), proposes that the pyramids were not built as pyramids at all. They were built as a series of sacrificial structures that were systematically cannibalized to produce the final form.

The construction sequence, as Choi reconstructed it, works as follows:

Step 1: Preparing the plateau. The builders identified and shaved the high points of natural limestone outcrops on the Giza plateau, terracing the landscape into a flat working platform. The plateau itself became the foundation, not just the site.

Step 2: Building a trapezoid, not a pyramid. Instead of attempting to build a pyramid shape from the start, the builders constructed a massive trapezoidal step-mass. This structure spreads outward at the top rather than tapering to a point. The key insight is that this shape integrates a workable, gentle ramp all the way to the full height of the structure, providing a wide, flat working deck at the apex with full visibility of all four edges. The builders were not building a pyramid. They were building a machine for building a pyramid.

Step 3: Establishing the apex first. On the flat deck at the top of the trapezoid, the builders placed the capstone, the pyramidion, at the precise mathematical center. This is the invariant reference point for everything that follows. The apex is established before a single stone of the final pyramid geometry is placed.

Step 4: Carving downward. With the apex fixed, the builders began dismantling the trapezoidal step-mass from the top down, shaving away the excess stone to reveal the final 51.84-degree pyramid geometry. Every stone placed in the final structure is placed in reference to the fixed, invariant apex. The ramp is not removed at the end. It is consumed in the process of building. The debris from the carving becomes the building material for the next course of casing stones.

Step 5: Cannibalization and the clean form. The entire trapezoidal scaffold is consumed as the pyramid is revealed. By the time the structure is complete, there is no ramp to remove, no scaffold to dismantle, no construction debris to dispose of. The evidence of construction does not disappear over time. It was erased by design. The builders intentionally cannibalized their own construction engine to leave behind a pure, sacred form with no trace of the human builders or their methods. This is why there is no archaeological evidence of construction infrastructure. It was never meant to survive.

The Missing Evidence, Explained

Every previous theory has struggled to explain the complete absence of construction evidence at Giza. Choi's theory inverts the problem: the absence of evidence is the evidence. The sacrificial structure was designed to be consumed. The ramp debris is missing because it became the casing stones. The internal spiral is missing because it was never built. The water canals are missing because they were never needed. The theory predicts the absence of evidence as a necessary consequence of the construction method.

The Keying Stones: Physical Fingerprint of the Method

The theory would be compelling on logical grounds alone. What makes it genuinely persuasive is that it makes a specific, testable prediction about physical evidence that should exist if the top-down carving method was used, and that evidence has been found.

The YouTube channel History for GRANITE, in their video "Closest Look Ever at How Pyramids Were Built," cataloged the approximately 6,000 surviving casing stones on the pyramids and identified a specific pattern among them: tapered bonding stones, which Choi calls keying stones, that appear at regular intervals in the surviving casing stone courses.

Keying stones are not decorative. They are structural artifacts of the top-down carving process. When you carve a pyramid geometry downward from a trapezoidal mass, the interface between the carved surface and the uncarved mass requires a specific type of bonding stone to maintain structural integrity at the boundary. The geometry of these stones, their taper angle, their placement interval, and their relationship to the surrounding courses, is a direct physical fingerprint of the carving direction. You cannot produce this pattern by building upward. The geometry is only consistent with downward carving from a fixed reference point.

The keying stone analysis is the physical evidence that distinguishes Choi's theory from a purely logical argument. It is the equivalent of finding tool marks that can only be produced by one specific technique. The full geometric analysis is documented in History for GRANITE's research and is available for independent verification.

Why This Is Not a Metaphor

When Glen described this theory, the connection to the trading card research was immediate. Both systems are governed by the same mathematical structure, derived independently from first principles in each domain.

In the pyramid, building from the bottom up means that every angular error in stone placement propagates forward. A deviation of one degree at the base becomes a deviation of many degrees by the time you reach the apex. The standard deviation of positional error grows as sqrt(k) with the number of courses k. Error compounds with every step, and by the time you reach the apex, you are so far from the intended geometry that the structure cannot be completed correctly.

Building from the top down, with the apex as the fixed reference point, means that every stone is placed in reference to the invariant structure. Errors do not accumulate. They are corrected at each step by reference to the fixed point. The standard deviation of error at any course is bounded by the single-step noise, regardless of how many courses have been placed.

Bottom-Up (Surface-First)

Every angular error propagates forward. Error at course k grows as sqrt(k). By the apex, accumulated error makes correct geometry impossible. The surface is the starting point; the deep structure is what you are trying to reach.

Top-Down (Structure-First)

Every stone references the fixed apex. Errors do not accumulate. Error at any course is bounded by single-step noise. The deep structure is the starting point; the surface is what emerges from it.

In the trading card market, analyzing from the surface inward means starting from the listed price, the asking price, the sentiment indicator, and trying to infer true value by adding inference steps. Every inference step adds error. The more steps you take, the more wrong you are. The error function takes the same mathematical form: uncertainty grows as you move further from the genuine transaction record.

Analyzing from the deep structure outward means starting from the genuine transaction record, the actual completed sales, the real interaction field, and letting the price be an output of that analysis. Uncertainty decreases as 1/sqrt(n) with the number of genuine transactions. The deep structure is the fixed reference point. The surface price is what emerges from it.

The same equation governs error behavior in both systems, derived independently from first principles in each domain. This is a structural correspondence, not a metaphor. We call it a structural correspondence rather than a formal isomorphism because a true mathematical isomorphism requires a fully specified structure-preserving bijection, which we have not established. What we have established is that the same equation governs error behavior in both systems.

The pyramid is not a metaphor for the market. The market is not a metaphor for the pyramid. Both are instances of the same underlying principle: systems that start from the deep structure and let the surface be an output are governed by bounded, correctable error. Systems that start from the surface and try to work inward are governed by compounding, uncorrectable error. The mathematical form is the same. The correction is the same. The principle generalizes.

The Backwards Problem, Defined

The Backwards Problem is what happens when you analyze a complex system from the surface inward rather than from the deep structure outward.

In the pyramid, the surface is the visible exterior. The deep structure is the fixed apex. Building from the surface inward means starting from the base and trying to reach the apex by accumulating courses of stone. Every error compounds. The further you go, the more wrong you are.

In the market, the surface is the listed price, the asking price, the sentiment indicator, the analyst estimate. The deep structure is the genuine transaction record, the actual completed sales, the real interaction field. Analyzing from the surface inward means starting from the listed price and trying to infer true value by adding inference steps. Every inference step adds error. The more steps you take, the more wrong you are.

This is not a mistake in judgment. It is not a failure of effort or intelligence. It is a structural feature of misoriented analysis. You can be extremely careful, extremely rigorous, and extremely well-resourced, and you will still compound error if you are starting from the wrong end. The Backwards Problem is not about being wrong. It is about being wrong in a way that gets systematically worse the harder you try, because every additional inference step from a misoriented starting point adds more error than it removes.

There is an important distinction between the Backwards Problem and ordinary measurement error. Ordinary measurement error is random. It does not have a direction. It averages out over many measurements. The Backwards Problem produces systematic, directional error that compounds rather than averaging out. The more carefully you measure from the wrong starting point, the more confidently wrong you become. This is why it is so dangerous: it is invisible from inside the misoriented framework. The system looks like it is working. The metrics are improving. The models are getting more sophisticated. The error is growing.

The correction is not complicated. Start from the deep structure. Let the surface variables be outputs of your analysis, not inputs. This sounds obvious once stated. The reason it is not universally practiced is that the deep structure is usually harder to access than the surface.

The listed price is right there. The genuine transaction record requires work to compile. The engagement metric is easy to measure. The genuine connection is hard to quantify. The test score is easy to produce. The actual understanding is hard to assess. The surface is always more legible than the deep structure. That is precisely why so many systems are oriented backwards. It is not laziness or incompetence. It is a rational response to a measurement problem that has the unfortunate property of compounding error at every step.

The Same Mistake Everywhere

Once you understand the structure of the Backwards Problem, you start seeing it in every complex domain you examine. The surface variable is always more legible. The deep structure is always harder to access. The analytical framework is almost always oriented from the surface inward. And the failure rate is always higher than it should be, in a specific mathematical way that reflects the compounding of error from a misoriented starting point.

Pharmaceutical Drug Discovery

The pharmaceutical industry starts from a molecular target, a surface variable representing a specific biological mechanism, and tries to infer which compounds will work in living biological systems, the deep structure of human physiology. The result is a 90% clinical failure rate across all drug candidates that enter Phase I trials. That is not bad luck. That is the Backwards Problem at industrial scale.

The compounds that look promising at the molecular level fail when they encounter the full complexity of the biological system they were supposed to interact with. The error compounds from the surface inward: a compound that binds to the target in vitro fails in cell culture, a compound that works in cell culture fails in animal models, a compound that works in animal models fails in human trials. Each step adds error because each step moves further from the surface variable and closer to the deep structure of actual human physiology. By the time you reach the clinical trial, you are so far from the deep structure that most of what you are testing was never going to work.

The human cost of this misorientation is not just financial. It is measured in years of trial participants exposed to compounds that had no realistic chance of succeeding, in the opportunity cost of research funding directed at surface-level optimization rather than deep-structure understanding, and in the decades of delay between scientific discovery and clinical benefit. We examine this in detail in Article 9.

Social Media Platforms

Social media platforms start from engagement metrics, clicks, likes, shares, watch time, and try to infer what users actually want from the platform. The result is a product that makes users feel worse over time, generates low-quality advertiser data, and is actively destroying its own long-term revenue base.

The platforms are not malicious. They are misoriented. They built their entire optimization stack from the surface inward, and the error has been compounding for fifteen years. Engagement is the surface variable. Genuine human connection, the actual reason people use social platforms, is the deep structure. Optimizing for engagement while ignoring genuine connection is the Backwards Problem applied to a two-sided market. The surface metric improves. The underlying value degrades. The users stay because the network effects are strong, but they are less happy, less connected, and less willing to pay for the experience. The advertiser data gets noisier because the users being served ads are increasingly disengaged from the platform's nominal purpose. The revenue model begins to fail.

The specific mechanism, the revenue model, and the proposed correction are examined in Article 3.

Education

Schools start from test scores and try to infer genuine understanding. Test scores are the surface variable. Genuine understanding, the ability to apply knowledge in novel contexts, to reason under uncertainty, to build on prior learning, is the deep structure. The result of optimizing for test scores is a system that produces students who are very good at taking tests and significantly less good at the things that tests are supposed to measure.

This is not a new observation. What is new is the mathematical framing: teaching to the test is not just a pedagogical failure. It is the Backwards Problem. Every optimization step that improves test scores without improving genuine understanding moves the system further from the deep structure. The error compounds. The gap between measured performance and actual capability grows. And because the surface metric keeps improving, the system looks like it is working right up until the moment when students encounter problems that tests cannot prepare them for.

Venture Capital

Venture investors start from pitch deck metrics, revenue projections, TAM calculations, and team credentials, and try to infer which founders will build something that matters. These are all surface variables. The deep structure is the founder's actual capacity to navigate the specific uncertainty of their market, to learn faster than the market changes, to build a team that compounds rather than fragments under pressure.

The result is a system with a failure rate that multiple studies place between 75% and 90% depending on how failure is defined, structurally similar to the pharmaceutical industry's failure rate, and for the same reason. The surface variables are legible. The deep structure is not. The analytical framework is oriented from the surface inward. The error compounds at every stage of due diligence, because every additional inference step from the pitch deck moves further from the actual question, which is not "does this business model look good on paper" but "can this specific person navigate this specific uncertainty."

The short version of everything in this series is this: most complex systems fail because they are analyzed from the wrong direction. The correction is to start from the deep structure and let the surface be an output. That is not a complicated idea. But the implications, once you follow them through, are surprisingly far-reaching.
