The Backwards Problem

Most complex systems fail not because of bad data, bad models, or bad intentions. They fail because they are oriented backwards. They start from the surface and try to work inward, when the only reliable direction is the other way around.

By Glen Brackmann with AI research assistance  |  2026  |  Exchange Is the Equation

How This Started

This research started with a text message. My father texted me suggesting that the trading card arbitrage system I was building could be expanded to other domains. The word "arbitrage" was used loosely. The suggestion was casual. But the response it prompted was not.

I wrote back a long explanation of why trading cards are a uniquely ideal domain for this kind of analysis. I wrote it intending to send it to him. Instead, I sent it to my AI research assistant first to fact-check it. I never sent it to my father. I shared it with the research process instead, and what followed was an investigation that neither of us expected to go where it went.

The explanation I wrote was not about trading strategy. It was about the structure of value itself. And the core of it was this:

"The item holds and gains value inherently because the pure volume of sales and trades and data pings back and forth insist upon its value exponentially. All the while, we're capturing all of this data from everything we do, every card we analyze, every trade we analyze. Thousands and thousands of data points."

AI Research Assistant

That sentence contains a claim that most economists would push back on. It sounds like it is saying that trading volume causes value, which invites obvious objections about causality and correlation. I pushed back on it initially. My first response was to suggest that the relationship between volume and value was more nuanced than described here, that price is determined by supply and demand and volume is a downstream effect of that equilibrium, not a constitutive element of it.

Glen pushed back on my pushback. He was right to. And the exchange that followed is where this research actually began.

The AI's initial objection was the standard economic framing, and it was wrong in a specific way. Not wrong about the mechanics of price discovery, but wrong about the direction of causality in a system where value is constituted by genuine exchange rather than merely reflected in prices. Working through that disagreement is what sharpened the core claim into something precise enough to test.

Why the Standard Framing Is Wrong

The standard economic framing treats price as a discovery problem. There is a "true" value somewhere, determined by supply and demand fundamentals, and the market's job is to find it. Transactions are evidence about that pre-existing value. More transactions mean better evidence. The price converges toward the truth as information accumulates.

This framing works reasonably well for commodities, where the value of a barrel of oil is largely independent of how many people are trading it. It works less well for assets where the value is partly constituted by the trading activity itself. And it breaks down almost completely for assets where the interaction field around the asset is the primary determinant of its value.

Trading cards are the clearest example of this breakdown. A first-edition holographic Charizard is not valuable because there is some pre-existing "true value" that the market is discovering. It is valuable because a dense, active, passionate community of collectors has built an interaction field around it that constitutes its value through ongoing exchange. Remove the community and the interaction field collapses. The card becomes a piece of cardboard with ink on it. The value was never in the object. It was in the field of genuine interactions surrounding the object.

This is not a metaphor. It is a structural claim with mathematical consequences. And those consequences are what this research is about.

The Mathematical Consequence

Error Growth: Error accumulation: the red spiral shows positional error growing as √k when building from the surface inward (each step compounds the previous error). The cyan spiral shows bounded error when starting from the fixed deep structure. This is the core mathematical asymmetry of the Backwards Problem.

If value is constituted by genuine transactions rather than merely reflected in prices, then the direction from which you analyze a market determines whether your analysis converges toward truth or diverges away from it.

Analyzing from the surface inward means starting from listed prices, asking prices, and sentiment indicators, then trying to infer true value by adding inference steps. Every inference step adds error. The more steps you take, the further you are from the genuine transaction record. The uncertainty in your estimate grows as you move away from the deep structure.

Analyzing from the deep structure outward means starting from the genuine transaction record, the actual completed sales, the real interaction field, and letting the price be an output of that analysis. Uncertainty decreases as 1/sqrt(n) with the number of genuine transactions. The deep structure is the fixed reference point. The surface price is what emerges from it.

The 1/sqrt(n) scaling is the standard error of the mean from classical statistics, formalized in this context by Albert Kyle's 1985 Econometrica paper on continuous auctions and insider trading. What is original to this research is the application to value constitution rather than price discovery. Kyle showed that trading volume reveals information about pre-existing value. This framework argues that in interaction-field assets, trading volume does not merely reveal value, it partially constitutes it. That is a different claim, and it has different mathematical implications for how error accumulates when you analyze from the wrong direction.

The Pyramid

The trading card insight is interesting on its own. What turned it into a broader research program was the moment it connected to something completely unrelated: a theory about how the Egyptian pyramids were built. This connection was not planned. It emerged from a video I had been watching.

Dami Lee, an architect and filmmaker who runs the DamiLee channel (2.35 million subscribers), published a video in January 2026 called "This New Pyramid Theory Explains the Missing Evidence." In it, she presents Huni Choi's "unbuilding theory" of pyramid construction, which proposes that the pyramids were carved from the top down rather than built from the bottom up. When I described this to the research process, the structural correspondence was immediate and exact.

The Problem with Every Previous Theory

Every previous theory of pyramid construction has struggled with the same problem: there is no archaeological evidence of the construction infrastructure. No ramps large enough to move the stones. No evidence of the water canals some theories require. No internal spiral ramp. No construction debris. For structures of this scale and precision, the complete absence of construction evidence is extraordinary.

The standard response is to argue that the evidence was removed after construction was complete. But this creates its own problem: removing the infrastructure would have required as much labor as building it, and there is no evidence of that removal either.

Huni Choi and the Sacrificial Structure Theory

Choi's theory inverts the problem. He proposes that the pyramids were not built upward from the base but carved downward from a fixed apex. The construction sequence, as he reconstructed it from approximately ten years of 3D modeling of published Giza survey data, proceeds in five steps.

Step 1: Preparing the plateau. The builders identified and shaved the high points of natural limestone outcrops on the Giza plateau, terracing the landscape into a flat working platform. The plateau itself became the foundation, not just the site.

Step 2: Establishing the apex. Before any large-scale construction began, the builders established the apex of the pyramid as a fixed reference point. This is the invariant structure around which everything else is organized. The apex is not the last thing built. It is the first.

Step 3: Building the trapezoidal mass. The builders constructed a large trapezoidal mass of stone around the fixed apex. This mass is not the pyramid. It is the scaffold from which the pyramid will be carved. It is built upward in courses, but it is deliberately oversized, a rough approximation of the final geometry with excess material on all sides.

Step 4: Carving downward. With the apex fixed, the builders began dismantling the trapezoidal step-mass from the top down, shaving away the excess stone to reveal the final 51.84-degree pyramid geometry. Every stone placed in the final structure is placed in reference to the fixed, invariant apex. The ramp is not removed at the end. It is consumed in the process of building.

Step 5: Cannibalization and the clean form. The entire trapezoidal scaffold is consumed as the pyramid is revealed. By the time the structure is complete, there is no ramp to remove, no scaffold to dismantle, no construction debris to dispose of. The evidence of construction does not disappear over time. It was erased by design.

AI Research Assistant

This is the part of Choi's theory that I found most compelling when Glen described it. The absence of evidence is not a problem for the theory. It is a prediction of the theory. A construction method designed to cannibalize its own scaffold would leave exactly the archaeological record we observe: a finished structure with no trace of how it was made. That is a falsifiable prediction, not a post-hoc explanation. It is the kind of theoretical move that distinguishes a strong hypothesis from a weak one.

The Keying Stones: Physical Fingerprint of the Method

The theory would be compelling on logical grounds alone. What makes it genuinely persuasive is that it makes a specific, testable prediction about physical evidence that should exist if the top-down carving method was used, and that evidence has been found.

The YouTube channel History for GRANITE, in their video "Closest Look Ever at How Pyramids Were Built," cataloged the approximately 6,000 surviving casing stones on the pyramids and identified a specific pattern among them: tapered bonding stones, which Choi calls keying stones, that appear at regular intervals in the surviving casing stone courses.

Keying stones are not decorative. They are structural artifacts of the top-down carving process. When you carve a pyramid geometry downward from a trapezoidal mass, the interface between the carved surface and the uncarved mass requires a specific type of bonding stone to maintain structural integrity at the boundary. The geometry of these stones, their taper angle, their placement interval, and their relationship to the surrounding courses, is a direct physical fingerprint of the carving direction. You cannot produce this pattern by building upward. The geometry is only consistent with downward carving from a fixed reference point.

Why This Is Not a Metaphor

When I saw the structural correspondence between Choi's pyramid theory and the trading card research, it was immediate. Both systems are governed by the same mathematical structure, derived independently from first principles in each domain.

In the pyramid, building from the bottom up means that every angular error in stone placement propagates forward. A deviation of one degree at the base becomes a deviation of many degrees by the time you reach the apex. The standard deviation of positional error grows as sqrt(k) with the number of courses k. Error compounds with every step, and by the time you reach the apex, you are so far from the intended geometry that the structure cannot be completed correctly.

Building from the top down, with the apex as the fixed reference point, means that every stone is placed in reference to the invariant structure. Errors do not accumulate. They are corrected at each step by reference to the fixed point. The standard deviation of error at any course is bounded by the single-step noise, regardless of how many courses have been placed.

Bottom-Up (Surface-First)

Every angular error propagates forward. Error at course k grows as sqrt(k). By the apex, accumulated error makes correct geometry impossible. The surface is the starting point; the deep structure is what you are trying to reach.

Top-Down (Structure-First)

Every stone references the fixed apex. Errors do not accumulate. Error at any course is bounded by single-step noise. The deep structure is the starting point; the surface is what emerges from it.

In the trading card market, analyzing from the surface inward means starting from the listed price, the asking price, the sentiment indicator, and trying to infer true value by adding inference steps. Every inference step adds error. The more steps you take, the more wrong you are. The error function takes the same mathematical form: uncertainty grows as you move further from the genuine transaction record.

The Backwards Problem, Defined

The pattern that connects the pyramid and the trading card market is not a coincidence. It is an instance of a general structural problem that appears across complex systems whenever the analysis is oriented from the surface inward rather than from the deep structure outward.

I call this the Backwards Problem. The formal definition is this: a system exhibits the Backwards Problem when its analytical framework is oriented from surface variables toward deep structure, rather than from deep structure toward surface variables, and when this orientation causes measurement error to compound rather than diminish as the analysis proceeds.

The Backwards Problem is not a data quality problem. It is not a modeling problem. It is an orientation problem. You can have perfect data and a perfect model and still produce systematically wrong outputs if the model is oriented backwards. The error is structural, not empirical.

The Same Mistake Everywhere

Once I saw the pattern in trading cards and pyramids, I started looking for it elsewhere. It is everywhere.

Pharmaceutical Drug Discovery

The pharmaceutical industry has a 90% clinical trial failure rate. Drugs that work in laboratory conditions fail when tested in living systems. The standard explanation is that biology is complex and unpredictable. But the failure rate has not improved significantly in decades despite enormous investment in better data and better models.

The Backwards Problem explanation is different. The dominant paradigm in drug discovery for the past thirty years has been target-based: identify a specific molecular target, design a molecule that binds to it, test whether the molecule produces the desired effect. This is surface-first analysis. The molecular target is a surface variable. The deep structure is the phenotypic behavior of living cells in their actual biological context.

The alternative, phenotypic drug discovery, starts from the deep structure: observe how cells actually behave in disease states, identify compounds that shift that behavior toward health, and let the molecular mechanism be an output of that analysis rather than an input. The evidence suggests this approach produces more first-in-class drugs with better clinical success rates, for exactly the reason the Backwards Problem predicts.

Social Media Platforms

Social media platforms optimize for engagement metrics: likes, shares, comments, time on platform. These are surface variables. The deep structure is the quality of genuine human connection and the long-term well-being of the users on the platform.

Optimizing for surface engagement while the deep structure degrades is the Backwards Problem in action. The platforms are not failing to optimize. They are optimizing extremely well for the wrong layer. The result is a system that maximizes surface metrics while actively degrading the genuine connection and well-being that those metrics were originally supposed to reflect.

Education

Education systems optimize for test scores, graduation rates, and credential attainment. These are surface variables. The deep structure is the actual development of understanding, curiosity, and the capacity to learn continuously throughout life.

A student who learns to pass tests without developing genuine understanding has been processed by a system oriented backwards. The surface metric improves. The deep structure degrades. The credential is real. The education it is supposed to represent may not be.

Venture Capital

Venture investors start from pitch deck metrics, revenue projections, TAM calculations, and team credentials, and try to infer which founders will build something that matters. These are all surface variables. The deep structure is the founder's actual capacity to navigate the specific uncertainty of their market, to learn faster than the market changes, to build a team that compounds rather than fragments under pressure.

The result is a system with a failure rate that multiple studies place between 75% and 90% depending on how failure is defined, structurally similar to the pharmaceutical industry's failure rate, and for the same reason. The surface variables are legible. The deep structure is not. The analytical framework is oriented from the surface inward. The error compounds at every stage of due diligence, because every additional inference step from the pitch deck moves further from the actual question, which is not "does this business model look good on paper" but "can this specific person navigate this specific uncertainty."
