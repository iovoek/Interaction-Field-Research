The Backwards Problem

Most complex systems fail not because of bad data, bad models, or bad intentions. They fail because they are oriented backwards. They start from the surface and try to work inward, when the only reliable direction is the other way around.

By Glen Brackmann with AI research assistance  |  2026  |  Exchange Is the Equation

This research started with a text message. Glen Brackmann's father texted him suggesting that the trading card arbitrage agent Glen was building could be expanded to other domains. The word "arbitrage" was used loosely. The suggestion was casual. But the response it prompted was not.

Glen wrote back a long explanation of why trading cards are a uniquely ideal domain for this kind of analysis. He wrote it intending to send it to his father. He sent it to me first to fact-check. He never sent it to his father. He shared it here instead, and what followed was a research process that neither of us expected to go where it went.

The explanation Glen wrote was not about trading strategy. It was about the structure of value itself. And the core of it was this:

"The item holds and gains value inherently because the pure volume of sales and trades and data pings back and forth insist upon its value exponentially. All the while, we're capturing all of this data from everything we do, every card we analyze, every trade we analyze. Thousands and thousands of data points."

That sentence contains a claim that most economists would push back on. It sounds like it is saying that trading volume causes value, which invites obvious objections about causality and correlation. I pushed back on it, initially. My first response was to suggest that the relationship between volume and value was more nuanced than Glen was describing, that price is determined by supply and demand and volume is a downstream effect of that equilibrium, not a constitutive element of it.

Glen pushed back on my pushback. He was right to. And the exchange that followed is where this research actually began.

Why the Standard Framing Is Wrong

The standard economic framing treats price as the output of a supply-demand equilibrium. In this framing, there is a "true" price that exists independently of whether anyone is actually trading. The market "discovers" this price through transactions, but the price itself is prior to the transactions. Volume is a byproduct of the equilibrium, not a component of it.

This framing works reasonably well for simple, liquid, commodity-like assets. It breaks down badly for complex assets, thin markets, and anything where value is not a fixed property of the object but an emergent property of the ecosystem around it.

A trading card is not a commodity. A first-edition holographic Charizard is made of cardboard and ink. The cardboard and ink are worth essentially nothing. What makes the card valuable is the entire ecosystem of collectors, traders, graders, tournament players, and speculators who have built a shared understanding of what that card represents. That shared understanding is not prior to the transactions. It is constituted by them. Every sale, every trade, every price check, every grading submission is a data point that reinforces or adjusts the collective understanding of what this object is worth.

Remove the transactions and you do not have an asset with a price that is hard to discover. You have an object with no price at all. The price does not exist somewhere waiting to be found. It comes into existence through the process of exchange. The exchange is the equation.

This is not a subtle semantic distinction. It is a structural claim about how value forms in complex systems, and it has consequences for every analytical framework that treats value as something to be measured rather than something to be constituted.

The Mathematical Consequence

If value is constituted by genuine transactions, then the uncertainty in a value estimate is a direct function of how many genuine transactions you have observed. The more transactions, the more the value has been constituted, and the less uncertain it is. This gives you a precise equation:

The Interaction Field Equation
SE(n) = σ / √n
Where n is the number of genuine transactions, σ is the intrinsic volatility of the asset, and SE(n) is the standard error of the value estimate. Uncertainty decreases as the square root of transaction count.

This is the standard error of the mean, applied to a new context. What is new is not the equation. What is new is the claim about what it is measuring: not a statistical estimate of a pre-existing price, but the actual formation of value through exchange.

The implication is that in thin markets, where n is small, value is genuinely uncertain. Not just hard to estimate. Genuinely uncertain, in the same way that a quantum state is genuinely uncertain before measurement. The uncertainty is not a measurement problem. It is a structural feature of the market at that stage of its development. And the way to reduce that uncertainty is not to find better data sources or build more sophisticated models. It is to wait for more genuine transactions to occur, or to be the analyst who understands that the uncertainty is real and prices it accordingly.

This is where the trading card market becomes analytically interesting. It has a wide range of market depths, from extremely thin (a few sales per year for obscure variants) to extremely liquid (thousands of sales per day for popular cards). This range lets you test the 1/sqrt(n) prediction across several orders of magnitude of transaction count. The prediction is that bid-ask spreads, price volatility, and value uncertainty should all scale as n^(-0.5) across this range. The empirical evidence supports this. The full test is documented in the evidence section of the main research site.

The Pyramid

The trading card insight is interesting on its own. What made it into a broader research program was the moment it connected to something completely unrelated: a theory about how the Egyptian pyramids were built.

The conventional assumption in Egyptology for most of the twentieth century was that the pyramids were constructed from the bottom up. Workers would lay the base courses of stone, build external ramps to drag subsequent courses upward, and progressively reduce the ramp as the structure rose toward the apex. This model has a well-known logistical problem: to maintain a workable grade as the structure approached its apex, the ramp itself would need to be larger than the pyramid. The math does not work.

The alternative theory, which has gained significant traction and is supported by physical evidence in the surviving casing stones, is that the pyramids were built from the top down. The capstone was placed first, on a prepared foundation. The structure was then built outward and downward from that fixed reference point, with each subsequent course of stone placed in relation to the invariant apex rather than in relation to the previous course.

The researcher Huni Choi developed what he calls the "unbuilding theory," documented by architect and YouTuber Dami Lee (Nollistudio) in her documentary series on pyramid construction. The specific pattern of tapered bonding stones, called keying stones, found among the surviving casing stones is a physical fingerprint of this top-down method. French architect Jean-Pierre Houbin independently proposed a related hybrid ramp theory supported by Egyptologist Bob Brier. These are not fringe ideas. They are serious architectural and archaeological proposals backed by physical evidence.

The mathematical consequence of the two construction methods is stark and exact:

Bottom-Up (Surface-First)

Every angular error in the placement of a stone propagates forward. A deviation of one degree at the base becomes a deviation of many degrees by the time you reach the apex. The standard deviation of positional error grows as sqrt(k) with the number of courses k. Error compounds with every step.

Top-Down (Structure-First)

Every stone is placed in reference to the fixed, invariant apex. Errors do not accumulate. They are corrected at each step by reference to the invariant structure. The standard deviation of error at any course is bounded by the single-step noise, regardless of how many courses have been placed.

The error function in the bottom-up model takes the same mathematical form as the trading card equation. In the pyramid, error grows as sqrt(k) with the number of courses. In the market, uncertainty decreases as 1/sqrt(n) with the number of transactions. These are the same equation, describing the same underlying phenomenon from two different directions. One system accumulates error by starting from the surface. The other eliminates error by starting from the deep structure.

This is not a metaphor. It is a structural correspondence between two independent systems governed by the same mathematical form. We call it a structural correspondence rather than a formal isomorphism because a true mathematical isomorphism requires a fully specified structure-preserving bijection, which we have not established. What we have established is that the same equation governs error behavior in both systems, derived independently from first principles in each domain.

The Backwards Problem, Defined

The Backwards Problem is what happens when you analyze a complex system from the surface inward rather than from the deep structure outward.

In the pyramid, the surface is the visible exterior. The deep structure is the fixed apex. Building from the surface inward means starting from the base and trying to reach the apex by accumulating courses of stone. Every error compounds. The further you go, the more wrong you are.

In the market, the surface is the listed price, the asking price, the sentiment indicator, the analyst estimate. The deep structure is the genuine transaction record, the actual completed sales, the real interaction field. Analyzing from the surface inward means starting from the listed price and trying to infer true value by adding inference steps. Every inference step adds error. The more steps you take, the more wrong you are.

This is not a mistake in judgment. It is not a failure of effort or intelligence. It is a structural feature of misoriented analysis. You can be extremely careful, extremely rigorous, and extremely well-resourced, and you will still compound error if you are starting from the wrong end. The Backwards Problem is not about being wrong. It is about being wrong in a way that gets systematically worse the harder you try, because every additional inference step from a misoriented starting point adds more error than it removes.

The correction is not complicated. Start from the deep structure. Let the surface variables be outputs of your analysis, not inputs. This sounds obvious once stated. The reason it is not universally practiced is that the deep structure is usually harder to access than the surface.

The listed price is right there. The genuine transaction record requires work to compile. The engagement metric is easy to measure. The genuine connection is hard to quantify. The test score is easy to produce. The actual understanding is hard to assess. The surface is always more legible than the deep structure. That is precisely why so many systems are oriented backwards. It is not laziness or incompetence. It is a rational response to a measurement problem that has the unfortunate property of compounding error at every step.

The Same Mistake Everywhere

Once you understand the structure of the Backwards Problem, you start seeing it in every complex domain you examine. The surface variable is always more legible. The deep structure is always harder to access. The analytical framework is almost always oriented from the surface inward. And the failure rate is always higher than it should be, in a specific mathematical way that reflects the compounding of error from a misoriented starting point.

In pharmaceutical drug discovery, the industry starts from a molecular target, a surface variable representing a specific biological mechanism, and tries to infer which compounds will work in living biological systems, the deep structure of human physiology. The result is a 90% clinical failure rate. That is not bad luck. That is the Backwards Problem at industrial scale. The compounds that look promising at the molecular level fail when they encounter the full complexity of the biological system they were supposed to interact with. The error compounds from the surface inward, and by the time you reach the clinical trial, you are so far from the deep structure that most of what you are testing was never going to work. The human cost of this misorientation is not just financial. It is measured in years of trial participants exposed to compounds that had no realistic chance of succeeding. We examine this in detail in the Article 9.

In social media, platforms start from engagement metrics, clicks, likes, shares, watch time, and try to infer what users actually want from the platform. The result is a product that makes users feel worse over time, generates low-quality advertiser data, and is actively destroying its own long-term revenue. The platforms are not malicious. They are misoriented. They built their entire optimization stack from the surface inward, and the error has been compounding for fifteen years. The specific mechanism, the revenue model, and the proposed correction are examined in Article 3 and the social media deep dive.

In education, schools start from test scores and try to infer genuine understanding. In urban planning, cities start from traffic flow models and try to infer how people actually want to live. In venture capital, investors start from pitch deck metrics and try to infer which founders will build something that matters. In each case, the surface variable is more legible, the analytical framework is oriented from the surface inward, and the failure rate is higher than it should be in a specific, predictable way.

The same mistake. The same mathematical structure. The same compounding error. The same correction available in every case: reorient the analysis to start from the deep structure and let the surface be an output.

What This Series Is

This is the first article in an eight-part series. Each article can be read independently, but they build on each other. The series moves from this origin and framing through the core theoretical framework, the most urgent applications, the formal mathematics, the empirical evidence, the failure conditions, and the open research questions.

The underlying research, with formal proofs, Monte Carlo simulations, and empirical tests across nine domains, is available at the Exchange Is the Equation research site. The articles here are the accessible version. They are written to be read by anyone who thinks carefully about how systems work, not just by people who can follow the mathematics.

The next article introduces the core framework in detail: what we mean by surface variables and deep structure, what makes a transaction genuine versus a surface signal, and how the Interaction Field forms around any complex asset or system. It is the conceptual foundation for everything that follows.

The short version of everything in this series is this: most complex systems fail because they are analyzed from the wrong direction. The correction is to start from the deep structure and let the surface be an output. That is not a complicated idea. But the implications, once you follow them through, are surprisingly far-reaching. The rest of this series follows them through.
