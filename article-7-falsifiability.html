How This Theory Could Be Wrong

Every serious theory must specify the conditions under which it would be falsified. Here are ours: the specific empirical tests that could disprove the Interaction Field Theory.

By Glen Brackmann with AI research assistance  |  2026  |  Exchange Is the Equation

A theory that cannot be falsified is not a scientific theory. It is a belief system. The Interaction Field Theory makes specific, quantitative predictions. Those predictions can be tested. If they fail, the theory is wrong, or at minimum, the assumptions need revision.

This article does not argue for the theory. It argues against it, as rigorously as possible. It presents the strongest objections, the most dangerous failure conditions, and the specific empirical tests that would disprove the core claims. If you are skeptical of the theory, this is the article to read first.

The Three Core Assumptions

The Orientation Theorem is proved under three assumptions. If any of these assumptions fails in a specific domain, the theorem does not apply to that domain.

A1: Independence. Transaction errors are independent of each other. If this fails, the sqrt(k) error growth formula changes. In markets with correlated errors (herding, cascades, contagion), the error may grow faster than sqrt(k). The theory would still hold directionally, but the quantitative predictions would be wrong.

A2: Invariance. The deep structure is stable relative to the timescale of analysis. If the deep structure itself is changing rapidly, starting from it provides no advantage over starting from the surface. In rapidly evolving markets or during structural breaks, this assumption may fail.

A3: Finite noise. The noise in each transaction is bounded. If noise is unbounded (fat-tailed distributions, black swan events), the central limit theorem arguments in the proof do not apply. The theory may still hold in expectation, but the variance of outcomes would be much larger than predicted.

Specific Falsification Tests

Test 1: The Forex Spread Prediction

The theory predicts that bid-ask spread scales as n^(-0.5) across currency pairs, where n is daily transaction volume. EUR/USD (billions of transactions per day) should have a spread approximately 1,000 times smaller than a currency pair with 1 million transactions per day. If the actual ratio is significantly different from sqrt(1,000,000,000 / 1,000,000) = 31.6, the quantitative prediction is wrong. This test is straightforward to run with publicly available forex data.

Test 2: The Social Media Revenue Crossover

The simulation predicts that connection-based optimization surpasses engagement-based optimization in monthly revenue at approximately Month 2, under the parameter assumptions of the model (v5, fully empirically anchored). If a platform ran a controlled experiment and the crossover did not occur within the range of Month 1-4, the model parameters would need significant revision. If the crossover never occurred (engagement-based optimization remained superior indefinitely), the core business case argument would be falsified.

Test 3: The Inflection Point Prediction

The theory predicts that the analytical edge of structure-first analysis is maximized near n_c, the inflection point of the logistic value formation curve. For trading cards, this means that the highest-return arbitrage opportunities should be concentrated in sets with transaction counts near n_c, not in the most liquid sets (where the edge is priced in) or the thinnest sets (where the signal is noise). If the distribution of arbitrage returns does not peak near n_c, the logistic curve model is wrong.

Test 4: The Phenotypic Screening Prediction

The theory predicts that phenotypic screening (structure-first) should have a higher success rate in clinical trials than target-based screening (surface-first). Swinney and Anthony (2011) found that phenotypic approaches produced more first-in-class drugs than target-based approaches in a historical analysis. If a prospective study found no difference in success rates between the two approaches, the pharmaceutical application of the theory would be falsified.

Test 5: The Dimensionality Scaling Prediction

The theory predicts that n_c scales with the number of independent value drivers d: n_c ~ O(d). Simple assets (sneakers, commodities) should reach price discovery at lower transaction counts than complex assets (stocks, real estate, drug candidates). If n_c does not scale with asset dimensionality, the logistic curve model needs revision. The StockX sneaker data already suggested this refinement is necessary: sneakers reached price discovery in the thin zone, not the inflection zone, consistent with low d.

The Goodhart's Law Problem

The social media application faces a specific challenge that deserves its own discussion. Goodhart's Law states: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. This applies directly to the proposed connection-based optimization. If platforms start optimizing for reciprocity index, conversation depth, and trust formation rate, bad actors will learn to fake these metrics. Coordinated inauthentic behavior, harassment disguised as conversation, and extremist communities that form genuine trust among members could all game connection metrics as easily as they game engagement metrics.

This is a real limitation. It does not falsify the theory, but it means that any implementation of connection-based optimization must be adversarially robust from the start. Single-metric optimization is always gameable. The solution is layered measurement, adversarial testing, and continuous recalibration. This is harder than optimizing for a single engagement metric, but it is the correct problem to be solving.

What the Theory Does Not Claim

The theory does not claim that surface variables are useless. They are downstream outputs of the interaction field and they carry real information. The claim is that they should be interpreted in the context of the deep structure, not used as the starting point for analysis.

The theory does not claim that structure-first analysis is always possible. In some domains, the deep structure is genuinely inaccessible. In those cases, surface-variable analysis is the best available option, and the theory's contribution is to quantify how much uncertainty that introduces.

The theory does not claim that the Backwards Problem is the only cause of failure in complex systems. It is one structural cause among many. Other causes (incentive misalignment, information asymmetry, coordination failures, resource constraints) operate independently and can produce similar failure patterns.
