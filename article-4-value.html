Value Is Not a Number. It Is a Process.

The difference between asking price, appraised value, and exchange-discoverable value. Why value becomes visible through genuine exchange, and what that means for every market.

By Glen Brackmann with AI research assistance  |  2026  |  Exchange Is the Equation

Ask someone what a house is worth. They will tell you a number. Ask them how they got that number. They will tell you about comparable sales, about what similar houses sold for recently, about what the Zestimate says, about what the appraiser concluded. Every answer is a reference to something else. No one can tell you, from first principles, what the house is worth. They can only tell you what other people have paid for similar things.

This is not a failure of knowledge. It is a feature of how value works. Value is not a property that objects possess independently of the humans who exchange them. Value is a process. It emerges from exchange. It is constituted by genuine transactions. Without transactions, there is no discoverable market-clearing price. There is only uncertainty.

The Five Kinds of Value

When people talk about "value," they are usually conflating at least five different things. The Interaction Field Theory distinguishes them precisely because confusing them is one of the primary ways the Backwards Problem enters a system.

TypeDefinitionExampleReliability
Asking PriceWhat a seller hopes to receiveeBay listing, real estate listingLow (noise)
Appraised ValueAn expert's estimate based on comparablesHome appraisal, card gradingMedium (depends on transaction density)
Sentimental ValueWhat the object means to a specific personFamily heirloom, childhood toyNot market-relevant
Use ValueWhat the object can do for you functionallyA hammer, a car, a drugDomain-specific
Exchange-Discoverable ValueWhat the object actually trades for in verified completed transactionsTCGPlayer completed sales, exchange-reported tradesHigh (signal)

The Interaction Field Theory is concerned with exchange-discoverable value. Not because the other types of value are unimportant, but because exchange-discoverable value is the only type that reduces uncertainty in a mathematically tractable way. Every genuine transaction provides new information about where the market-clearing price actually is. Every listing, appraisal, or sentiment is just a guess.

The Entropy Argument

In information theory, entropy is a measure of uncertainty. Maximum entropy means maximum uncertainty: you know nothing about the outcome. Zero entropy means zero uncertainty: you know exactly what the outcome will be.

The entropy argument for the Interaction Field Theory is this: in a market with zero genuine transactions, the exchange-price uncertainty is at maximum. You do not know what the asset will trade for because it has never traded. Every genuine transaction reduces that uncertainty. The more transactions, the lower the entropy, the closer you are to a stable, discoverable market-clearing price.

This is not the same as saying that an asset with no transactions has no value. It has no discoverable market-clearing price. It may have sentimental value, use value, or potential exchange value. But until it trades, you cannot know what it will trade for. The exchange is not evidence of the value. The exchange is the process by which value becomes discoverable.

The Logistic Curve

The relationship between transaction count and value certainty follows a logistic curve, not a linear one. This is one of the most important and most underappreciated aspects of the theory.

A linear relationship would mean that each additional transaction reduces uncertainty by the same amount. But that is not how markets work. The first few transactions in a new market are enormously informative. They establish the rough range within which the asset trades. The next few transactions narrow that range. But after a certain point, additional transactions provide diminishing returns. The market has converged. Additional transactions confirm the established price rather than discovering new information about it.

The inflection point of this logistic curve, which we call n_c, is the transaction count at which information gain per transaction is maximized. Assets near n_c are in the zone of maximum analytical edge. The market has enough transactions to establish a real signal, but not so many that the signal is fully priced in. This is where the Interaction Field is most powerful as an analytical tool.

What This Means for Markets

The practical implication is direct. In any market, the assets with the highest analytical edge are those near their inflection point n_c. In trading cards, this means sets that have enough sales data to establish a real price signal, but are not yet fully liquid. In real estate, this means neighborhoods that are transitioning from thin to active markets. In venture capital, this means companies that have completed enough funding rounds to establish a real valuation signal, but are not yet public.

The assets that are most dangerous to analyze using surface variables are those at the extremes: very thin markets (where the surface variable is pure noise) and very liquid markets (where the surface variable is fully priced in and the analytical edge is zero). The Backwards Problem is most costly in thin markets, where the temptation to use surface variables is highest and the error from doing so is largest.
